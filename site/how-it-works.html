From a file to a post

What happens, in the order it happens.

  1. Name the browser

    Every browser that uses flock claims a name once, so the log can say which machine made each change. Nothing can be uploaded or changed until it has one.

  2. Compose, one platform at a time

    Tick the platforms this video goes to. Each has its own text: a title and a description for YouTube, a caption for TikTok and Instagram. Templates drop reusable blocks in, and adaptation rules, replace this with that on this platform, run exactly once, at confirmation.

  3. Choose the video

    Drop a file on the picker, or pick one out of a watch folder on the NAS. A NAS file is referenced where it lies rather than transferred through the browser, which is how a large video is published.

  4. Make the per-post choices

    YouTube: playlist, thumbnail and paid promotion. TikTok: who can view the video, whether viewers may comment, duet or stitch, and the commercial-content disclosure. All of these are chosen on this upload, from what the account offers, with nothing preselected.

  5. Schedule

    One card per platform on a week grid. Drag it to a day and hour, type an exact time, pick a saved release pattern, or release immediately.

  6. Confirm

    The video plays beside what each platform will receive and when. The confirm button has to be pressed twice within two seconds. Nothing has left the machine before this point; on confirming, the video is stored and one release per platform is queued.

  7. The worker publishes

    A worker process beside the database watches the queue. YouTube is handed the video early and releases it itself at the slot. TikTok cannot schedule, so the worker holds the post until its slot, then reads the creator's details, uploads the file in chunks and polls TikTok until the post is live. Instagram is uploaded ahead of time and published on the minute. Every outcome, including a failure and its reason, is written back to the release and to the log.

  8. Connecting an account

    Each platform is connected once, from the terminal, through that platform's own consent page: Google's for YouTube, TikTok's Login Kit for TikTok, Instagram Login for Instagram. The resulting tokens are kept in a configuration file on the author's hardware and refreshed by the worker. Revoking access on the platform ends it.

The TikTok integration, call by call

What flock asks TikTok for, and when.

Login Kit
The authorization code flow with PKCE, requesting user.info.basic and video.publish. The refresh token is stored locally and rotated by the worker.
Before every post: creator info
/post/publish/creator_info/query/ returns the account's display name, the audiences it may post to, whether comments, duets and stitches are switched off in its settings, and the longest video it may post. The post screen offers only what this returns, and refuses a video longer than the limit.
Posting: direct post by file
/post/publish/video/init/ with the caption, the chosen audience, the interaction flags, the cover time and the disclosure toggles, and FILE_UPLOAD as the source. The file is then sent to the upload address TikTok returns, in chunks of 5 to 64 MB.
Afterwards: status
/post/publish/status/fetch/ every ten seconds until TikTok reports the post complete or failed. The outcome, and TikTok's reason if it failed, is recorded on the release.
Not used
video.upload, which sends a draft to the creator's inbox, is not requested by anything in the app. Nothing reads the account's videos, followers, statistics or messages.

Requirements

  • Node 22 or newer and pnpm, for the worker and the build.
  • A PocketBase instance of its own, which stores the videos and the queue and serves the app.
  • A machine that stays on, a NAS here, for the database and the worker.
  • A browser on the same network. flock is not reachable from the public internet.
